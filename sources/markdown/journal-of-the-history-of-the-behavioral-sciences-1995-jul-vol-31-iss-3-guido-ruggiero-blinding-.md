Throughout this period the social survey movement and government statistical agen- 
cies seemed blissfully ignorant of the importance of probability sampling and related 
survey methods. At the beginning of the Great Depression of the 1930s in the United 
States, however, the federal statistical agencies had difficulty responding to the demand 
for statistics to monitor the effects of the programs of President Franklin Roosevelt’s 
New Deal. In 1933, Secretary of Labor Frances Perkins asked Stuart A. Rice, the 
American Statistical Association’s president, to set up an advisory committee on the 
programs of the Bureau of Labor Statistics. This committee became the Committee on 
Government Statistics and Information Services (COGSIS), sponsored jointly by ASA 
and the Social Science Research C~uncil.~ 
COGSIS helped to stimulate the use of 
probability sampling methods in various parts of the federal government, and it helped 
to organize an experimental “Trial Census of Unemployment” as a Civil Works Ad- 
ministration project in three cities using probability sampling, carried out in late 1933 
and early 1934. The positive results from this study led in 1940 to the first large-scale, 
ongoing sample survey on employment and unemployment using probability sampling 
 method^.^ Here we see what I believe is the real reason for the demise of the local social 
survey-it failed to adopt the scientific method of probability sampling’ and, as a con- 
sequence, its results were not generalizable to the nation as a whole. The chapter by 
Roger Davidson, on the social survey in government perspective, misses this develop- 
ment completely, and other chapters mistakenly attribute the adoption of probability 
sampling to the political pollsters and media researchers of the 1930s. 
The Social Survey in Historical Perspective is both a success and a failure. Its suc- 
cess comes in capturing the energies and efforts of the leaders of the early social survey 
movement, setting their work in a carefully constructed social context. The chapters 
describing the early local social surveys are rich in detail and offer many insights on 
the later development of survey methods. The failure of the book derives in part from 
this strength and in part from an outlook shared by many of the chapter authors that 
focuses on forced social explanations for the decline and ultimate demise of the local 
social survey, and misses the alternate explanations for the rise of the modern large- 
scale sample survey that most of us now take for granted.

In his most recent book, Guido Ruggiero artfully uses material culled from the civic 
and ecclesiastical records of sixteenth-century Venice to explore what he terms the “poetics 
of the everyday” (223). This image and that embedded in that title-“binding passions”- 
are the central metaphors of the book. Ruggiero deploys both in a self-conscious at- 
tempt to evoke a world of ordinary social relations and its concommitant pressures as 
seen through the extraordinary lens of complaints lodged with the Holy Office and 
equivalent secular bodies such as the ,4vogadori di Comun and Esecutori contra la 
Bestemmia, all concerned with regulating the world of unorthodox and irregular behavior 
in the age of the Catholic Reformation. 
Following the chronology laid out by historians of popular culture such as Peter 
Burke, Ruggiero also sees the second half of the sixteenth century as an important turn- 
ing point in the interaction between popular and learned traditions.’ Yet Ruggiero does 
not rest his argument upon generalities, shaping it instead from his formidable knowledge 
of the Venetian archives. His stories chart the progressive establishment of an elaborate 
bureaucracy designed to regulate moral and religious behavior and to inspect more 
carefully the private lives of the Venetians. As historians such as Carlo Ginzburg have 
already demonstrated, these records provide a particularly interesting window on the 
activities of people whose lives might otherwise have remained obscure.2 Through Rug- 
giero’s tales we are introduced to a variety of rather unorthodox characters whose at- 
tempts to bind the passions of others brought them to the attention of the officials con- 
cerned with keeping order in the city and power in the hands of the church and state. 
The five chapters in Binding Passions introduce readers to a rich and socially com- 
plex world, filled with courtesans, women healers, problematic clerics, thwarted lovers, 
and the ubiquitous officials who persist in asking them questions. Commanding a wide 
array of popular religious and magical practices - from casting beans and creating love 
amulets to signing away illness and foretelling the future-they attempt to shore up their 
own positions in society. Concerns about honor, status and reputation inform the actions 
and the language of all of these individuals. While historians of early modern Europe 
have long been aware of the growing self-consciousness about social behavior among 
courtiers and patricians, Ruggiero convincingly demonstrates that popolani also shared 
these concerns. A courtesan could not afford to be considered a prostitute; a marriage 
broker would lose clients were she to be thought a witch. Conversely, women healers 
gained in reputation when they acted in the role of informal priests; in the case of the 
fortune-telling friar Aurelio di Siena, his insistence on wearing the robes of a cleric surely 
enhanced his authority among his clients. Time and time again, ordinary citizens ex- 
hibited a highly refined understanding of social, professional, and religious hierarchies, 
and used them to carve their place in the Republic. 
One of the great strengths of Ruggiero’s approach to his material comes from his 
interest in telling as many different sides of the story as his sources allow. In contrast 
to some of the early microhistories that drew fairly sharp distinctions between learned 
and popular culture, Binding Passions focuses on the ambiguous interactions between 
high and low. A number of his case studies indicate how highly regarded some of these 
so-called prostitutes, popular healers, and magicians were by the local elite (who often 
protected them, mitigated their punishments, and eagerly sought their services). Others 
suggest that even poor inhabitants might denounce a suspect neighbor. In short, there 
is no consistent element of class solidarity, although social and political conventions 
certainly prevailed in sorting out the reliability of accounts once a case was brought 
before the magistrates. While an astrologer or a courtesan might escape punishment -

Ruggiero is rightfully cautious about overgeneralizing social behavior from a handful 
of cases. Yet the broad contours that he sketches are quite interesting. First, he suggests 
the importance of marrying well at all levels in society. In his account, sixteenth-century 
Italians seem willing to use a variety of different strategies - political, familial, religious, 
and magical - to achieve a good end. From this perspective, magical practices become 
simply part of a spectrum of social strategies to perpetuate family and personal honor. 
Second, he envisions each local community as having some sort of nexus of power that re- 
volves around the supernatural. In urban centers that power might be closely, though not 
exclusively, linked to the church and its officials, particularly as religious authorities began 
to curtail the activities of their competitors. In more rural settings, however, the “power 
of rural women over things sacred” seems to have prevailed (132). This was a fact even 
acknowledged by certain theologians who, quite remarkably, viewed the powers of these 
women “priests” as greater than those of the ancients who had created the corpus of 
occult writings that formed the natural magic tradition. Contrary to Ginzburg, Rug- 
giero does not see these activities as vestigies of paganism but as a possible continuation 
of early Christian practices that gave women a wider role in the arts of caring and healing. 
For Ruggiero, magic is a form of self-assertion on the part of largely marginalized 
figures and social groups. Through such practices, one retained a claim on the sacred 
that was largely evaporating in the face of official encroachments into this realm. In 
his analysis of depositions and trial records, Ruggiero charts an increased awareness 
on the part of accused about the stakes involved in sorting out orthodox and unorthodox 
behavior. One of the best examples concerns the case of the women priests of Latisana 
who claim that being questioned and released by the church entails a form of licensing 
of their practices (since the church officials declared them licentiata to indicate their ac- 
quittal). In a sublime act of reinterpretation, such women used public knowledge of 
their release and the ambiguity of official language to claim greater authority and 
legitimacy in their practices. 
There are many commendable aspects of Ruggiero’s Binding Passions. The stories 
are deftly told, impressively documented and all work toward the larger goal of attend- 
ing to the vicissitudes of power in an age of renewed faith and restored discipline. My 
only real criticism concerns the ending of the book which is heavily laden with familiar 
echoes of the “world we have lost” and a nostalgia for a reenchanted world in a post- 
industrial age. Certainly these sentiments are heartfelt on the part of the author (and 
many of his readers). But it seems a pity to let the power of the supernatural fall away 
so abruptly after demonstrating its centrality to the world of the late Renaissance. This 
has the unfortunate effect of leaving us still enmeshed in the problems that we have 
been debating ever since the publication of Keith Thomas’s magisterial work on this 
same subject in Protestant England.3 One can only hope that Ruggiero will continue 
to explore these themes in future research and perhaps answer the interesting questions 
he has posed in this provocative and entertaining book.
