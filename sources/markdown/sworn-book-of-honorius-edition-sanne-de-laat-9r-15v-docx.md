Introduction to the text 
The Sworn Book of Honorius 
The Sworn Book of Honorius is a text on (theurgic) magic, most notably the 
construction of the Sigil of God (sigillum Dei) and how to obtain the beatific vision. Theurgic 
magic tend to involve “ritual to effect the soul’s purification, […] fellowship with 
intermediary beings (gods, angels, daemones)” and are oriented towards “revelation, or 
experiences in which something is transmitted by divine powers,” and are often based on 
angel magic, as Fanger also calls it (16). 
The manuscript used for this edition is the Royal 17 A XLII, which currently resides 
in the British Library. The catalogue dates it to the fifteenth century (Hedegård 16), but 
Mathiesen says the text has to be from the sixteenth century because parts of Agrippa’s De 
Occulta Philosophia have been copied in this text (Mathiesen 145). 
The source text for the Sworn Book of Honorius is the Liber iuratus Honorii (LiH). 
The Latin version can be traced back to the thirteenth century. Kieckhefer thinks it likely to 
have started in the early fourteenth century (“Devil’s contemplatives” 253-4), while 
Mathiesen instead says that LiH dates back to the first half of the thirteenth century, based on 
the idea that the LiH is actually the Sacred Book William of Auvergne references (146-7). 
Both Klaassen and Mesler agree with Kieckhefer by saying the tradition started in the 
fourteenth century – Klaassen says it might be the end of the thirteenth century as well 
(Mesler 113; Klaassen 102). Hedegård believes the date of composition of the Liber Iuratus 
Honorii to be somewhere in the early fourteenth century (13).  
However, Veenstra provides the clearest picture after the discovery of the Summa 
Sacre Magice. He says that there are two different traditions, instead, and that the Spanish 
tradition (so the version of the LiH which was used for the Summa) predates the London 
tradition (Veenstra 77). His dating of the LiH tradition is thirteenth or fourteenth century, but 
with the disclaimer that it could have been around in the 1230s when William of Auvergne 
was around – but the Liber iuratus is not the Liber sacratus as Mathiesen still believed 
(Veenstra 151-2). 
As mentioned before, the Sworn Book is part of the tradition of the LiH, while it also 
has some influences from Agrippa’s De Occulta. Hedegård mentions that the Sworn Book 
seems to be copied from Sloane 3854, but that it’s even more likely that it has been copied

Sigillum Dei 
Most of this part of the manuscript of the Sworn Book deals with making and 
consecrating the sigillum Dei, the sigil of God. The sigil needs to be made to make sure you 
can “perform the operations, which afterwards will be revealed in this sacred book” (Peterson 
Sworn Book 73). 
The description of how to make it is rather complicated, and on top of that, the scribe 
of the Royal manuscript missed two lines while copying the text – so technically, this text can 
never be used to make a complete sigil of God. While some of the instructrions are unclear 
and vague, the final form of the sigil should something like the one pictured below. This 
image is featured on the jacket of the edition Peterson published, which comes closest to 
looking the way the sigil is described in the edition, with correct colouring. 
 
 
Schemhamphoras

Lead and Concealed in Stone” by Chardonnens and Veenstra, the seventy-two names are 
listed, and it’s this list that I have taken as the ‘baseline’ for the Schemhamphoras. While 
Peterson did make some corrections in his edition, none of these were to the first letter of the 
names (Sworn Book 31). The source of this version is Ganell’s Summa, a text Veenstra

The Latin edition is rather clearly connected to the Summa, while the English edition 
is bad overall. There might be some copying mistakes, but even when added letters are left 
out, the ending does not make any sense (an ending in “s/m, y, m, a” instead of “e, y, o, t”). 
While this is an interesting subject, especially considering the dating for this 
manuscript, it still remains a mess, and one that cannot be solved easily. One could write a 
thesis on this subject. In the edition, the Schemhamphoras is kept the way it appeared in the 
manuscript as the mistakes have already been introduced here. Letters which were added to 
the Schemhamphoras in the manuscript, either as corrections or as additions, are put in the 
footnotes regardless of the fact if they are ‘right’ corrections. 
 
Fumigations 
While the fumigations only make a brief appearance towards the end of this part of 
the text, they are still a rather unknown concept. Fumigations are ingredients that are burned 
and of which the smokes (fumes) act as incense (Kieckhefer Magic 133). Fumigation was 
originally something that featured in astral magic, but was slowly taken over into other forms 
of magic (Kieckhefer Magic 166). 
The word ‘thimiamate’ is often found in this context. This is, according to the Sepher 
Raziel, “confections of good odours with which thou shalt suffume & thou shalt please to 
Creacion & thou shalt attayne what thou wilt by this” (Peterson Sepher Raziel 142r). The 
Latin word ‘thimiama’ is a transcription of the Greek word θυμίαμα, which means “a 
composition for fumigating, incense.” So, thimiamate means the good smells, or the incense 
you have to burn on that specified day. 
  
Editorial procedure 
The red text is coloured in the manuscript, even though it is unclear which colour it is 
in. All abbreviations have been silently expanded throughout the text. Full stops around 
numbers have been removed completely, while those around words, syllables and single 
letters have been replaced by quotation marks. The only exception to this is the 
Schemhamphoras, where the full stops have been kept to increase readability. Square 
brackets have been used to add missing lines and words in the text for the construction of the 
sigil of God and at the beginning and end of this part of the Sworn Book for added legibility. 
Those have been taken from the Peterson’s online edition of the Sworn Book on the Esoteric 
Archives.

Commas and full stops have been added for readibility, and capitalization has been 
modernized. Apart from that, spelling has been kept as it was in the manuscript. 
The translations of the Latin text were taken from Peterson’s printed edition of The 
Sworn Book of Honorius. Corrections have been made based on Peterson’s printed version 
and Hedegard’s Liber Iuratus Honorii. He used both Hedegård’s edition and his own 
transcription to come to the edition and its translation, and there are only a few differences 
between this version and the version I have transcribed. I followed Peterson in most issues 
with Latin, so mistakes in the Sworn Book have been corrected to Peterson for the most part, 
but have been accounted for in the footnotes. The translations are Peterson’s with the 
exception of a few mistakes in his translation which have been corrected in this edition. The 
translations of the Latin have been provided on the left page of the Latin itself. The format of 
the Latin text has slightly been changed to accommodate easier reading of the English 
translation: the English translation now starts on the same line as the corresponding Latin 
lines.
