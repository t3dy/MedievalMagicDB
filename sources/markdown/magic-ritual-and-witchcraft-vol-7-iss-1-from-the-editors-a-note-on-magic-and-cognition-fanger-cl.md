This study by J. Rousse-Lacordaire concerns Pedro Garsia, a Spanish
bishop and theologian (d. 1505) whose fame rests mainly on his late-
ﬁfteenth-century controversy with Giovanni Pico della Mirandola (1463–
94). Published almost three years after Pico’s Conclusiones (Dec. 1486), Gar-
sia’s Determinationes magistrales appeared with the same Roman publisher, E.
Silber, in October 1489, thus shortly following Pico’s Apologia, which stirred
the wrath of Pope Innocent VIII upon its appearance in May 1487. It is

indeed at the Pope’s request that Garsia composed his work—a determinatio
magistralis being a scholastic exercise designed to provide the basis, through a
closely argued discussion, for a doctrinal statement and/or decision. In the
present case, the chosen title was obviously meant to counter the proclaimed
‘‘Parisian style’’ of Pico’s Apologia and, at the same time, to refute some of
Pico’s Theses (or Conclusiones).
Rousse-Lacordaire’s effort provides us with the transcription, critical edi-
tion, and French translation of Garsia’s eleventh determinatio, which centers
on Pico’s ninth ‘‘magical conclusion according to his own opinion’’: Nulla
est sciencia, que nos magis certiﬁcet de divinitate Christi, quam magia et cabala. Given
this openly provocative formulation, it was somewhat inevitable that this
thesis in particular would entail controversy, and would end up being featured
among the thirteen ‘‘conclusions’’ eventually condemned. The present criti-
cal edition of the nineteen chapters composing the text of Garsia’s determinatio
is accompanied by an excellent, very precise translation and is also equipped
with careful and accurate annotations.
In a lengthy and erudite introductory study, the author makes evident what
basically constitutes the crux of the controversy between Garsia and Pico: a
complete misunderstanding about what is in fact at stake when using the
terms and notions of ‘‘nature,’’ ‘‘magic,’’ and ‘‘science.’’ Pico’s controversial
statement meant in fact that mastery over natural philosophy—including the
possession of the summae scientiae of magic and kabbalah—could only conﬁrm
that Christ’s miracles implied a level of accomplishment that far outreached
that of any sort of natural means (even enhanced by magic and kabbalah),
and therefore evidenced divine intervention. Garsia, however, stubbornly yet
competently stood by the Aristotelian and Thomist worldview. Within this
latter conceptual frame, magic is either linked to doubtful entities or—as
‘‘natural magic’’—is endowed with much more restricted operative powers
(if any at all) than are claimed within the early forms of Renaissance philo-
sophia perennis, of which the young Pico was of course an enthusiastic expo-
nent.
Pico’s conception of magic as the apex of scientia naturalis, and of kabbalah
as linked—under some particular aspects—to the practical application(s)
thereof, not only raises the question of what scope he really attributes to the
word (and concept) of ‘‘magic’’ as such, but also manifests the ambiguities of
his use of terms such as ‘‘science’’ or ‘‘nature.’’ In his Oratio and Conclusiones,
it is more or less made clear that ‘‘nature’’ reaches up to the threshold of the
divine realm (including the angelic spheres) and that ‘‘magic’’ tends to put
on, accordingly, a theurgic garb. This is quite in tune with the contents of
Ficino’s translations of some of the late Neoplatonic philosophers, such as

principally by backtracking somewhat on some of his more risque´ statements

Needless to say, this rather evasive presentation of ‘‘spiritual’’ magic, and
its inclusion within an entirely Christianized process of self-and cosmic divi-
nization modeled on a broad Neoplatonic worldview, is as lost on Garsia as
it is alien to his intellectual stance. Magic, for him, cannot possibly meddle
with spiritual transformation, which is exclusively dependent on religion and
on theology, obviously the one and only ‘‘science’’ to which a ‘‘revealed’’
character may correctly be attributed. In order for magic to retain even bor-
derline acceptance, its effects should be limited strictly to natural causes of the
kind outlined by Aristotle and the medieval doctors who dealt with natural
philosophy. As far as Garsia himself is concerned, magic’s potential efﬁcacy
can only rest on demonic agency, and it is therefore considered by him as
entirely illicit, assuming it has any use at all. If not exclusively angelic, no
‘‘spiritual’’ agency of any kind may be tolerated, and the additional mixing-
up of doctrines and material pertaining to ancient Greek, Jewish, or Arabic
sources with Christian tenets is to him equally suspicious, and ultimately
unacceptable from a theological perspective.
On top of its brilliant qualities of erudition and historical ﬁnesse, the pres-
ent study exhibits a perfect sense of balance in its analysis of both Pico and
Garsia, and competently refrains from either over- or understating one writ-
er’s theories in favor of the other’s. A case in point is the difﬁcult section on
the controversy concerning the question of the voces, and on the role of
language (Hebrew, essentially) and kabbalah in the practical aspects of magic.
Given Garsia’s own outlook, the consistency and effectiveness of his scholas-
tic arguments against Pico’s subtle (but sometimes obscure) developments are
well illustrated. In this instance, as in many others of the particular determinatio
at hand, Garsia’s theological competence and logical qualities are given a fair
