RICHARD KIECKHEFER, Magic in the Middle Ages. (Cambridge Medieval Textbooks.) Cam-
bridge, Eng.: Cambridge University Press, 1989. Pp. x, 219; 19 black-and-white illus-
trations. $39.50 (cloth); $9.95 (paper).
In the beginning of his Magic in the Middle Ages, Richard Kieckhefer asserts that he has
written this work as a "rounded survey . . . for an undergraduate audience," with the
additional hope that "others as well may find the book useful." In doing so Kieckhefer
has undertaken the ambitious project of describing both learned and folk magic in the
Middle Ages, while also attempting to cover their persecution. The result, while perhaps
useful for an undergraduate audience, suffers from basic flaws.
While Kieckhefer seems to be at home in the areas of folk magic and the persecution
of magicians, his treatment of the learned magical tradition is curt and sometimes mis-
leading. This is revealed early on by his odd choice of two fifteenth-century manuscript
compilations to illustrate the character of medieval magic. Why did he not choose one
of the classics of the genre, such as the De radiis of al-Kindi, the divinatory treatise of
Artephius, or the Liber vaccae of Pseudo-Plato, instead of two works that are not properly
medieval? In fact, Artephius and the Liber vaccae go unmentioned in Kieckhefer's book,
and al-Kindi's treatise, which formed a major inspiration of Giles of Rome's De erroribus
philosophorum, receives but one reference in the entire text.
A similar situation rules in Kieckhefer's treatment of alchemy. He starts well enough,
giving a brief description of Pseudo-Albertus Magnus's Semita recta, but then jumps into
a four-page analysis of the fifteenth-century British alchemist Thomas Norton. Meanwhile
he has failed to say anything of such absolutely fundamental texts as the Summa perfectionis
of Pseudo-Geber or the Testamentum of Pseudo-Lull. This may seem a pardonable over-
sight if one is unaware of the great importance of the Geberian and Lullian traditions
to alchemy in the Middle Ages, but in a survey that purports to cover that area it is far
from satisfactory.
All of this is not to say that Magic in the Middle Ages is without merit. The work contains
much interesting and valuable information in Kieckhefer's depiction of "the clerical
underworld," and his treatment of "the art of trickery" tackles a peculiar segment of

ELLEN E. KITTELL, From Ad Hoc to Routine: A Case Study in Medieval Bureaucracy. (Middle
Ages Series.) Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania Press, 1991. Pp. xii, 265; 2
figures, 6 tables, map. $36.95.
Historians have long recognized that the routinization of occasional administrative acts
can lead eventually to the creation of office. The personnel and physical resources at-
tendant on the new official constitute the basic building blocks of bureaucracy. In this
study of the general receivership of Flanders between 1262 and 1372 Ellen Kittell de-
lineates the various phases whereby the ad hoc performance of certain financial tasks
(receiving, disbursing, and accounting for funds) by a servant of the Flemish counts
became more repetitive, specialized, and systematized, coalescing into an institution. By
the end of the period under investigation the author discerns within the general receiv-
ership certain features characteristic of impersonal office: specific competence for a well-
defined set of duties, the capability to delegate authority to one's own deputies, insti-
tutional continuance beyond the actual incumbency of a specific person, formal ac-
countability, a fixed salary (instead of land grants, money fiefs, or cash gifts), and the
capacity to absorb new administrative responsibilities—even in areas unrelated to the
official's initial competence.
While investigating the various phases of the general receiver's institutional develop-
ment, Kittell also endeavors to place this process in a context of Flemish comital "state-
building." Along the way, the reader learns much about Flanders in the late thirteenth
and fourteenth centuries. Intense urbanization and the growth of Europe's first mass-
market light industry (the production of cheap woolen cloth) had made the Flemish
counts rich indeed. The management of such an income had contributed to a marked
financial precocity among the count's local agents (bailiffs and receivers). After 1262 the
counts faced two formidable political rivals: an expansionist French monarchy and po-
litically self-conscious urban communes. The latter were led by their richest members,
the "three cities" (drie stederi) of Ghent, Ypres, and Bruges.
The author explores—wholly or in part—the reigns of five Flemish rulers through six
generations: Margaret of Constantinople (d. 1278), Guy of Dampierre (d. 1305), Robert
of Bethune (d. 1322), Louis of Nevers (d. 1346), and Louis of Male (d. 1384). Her
specific chronological framework ranges from the first activity (in 1262) of the earliest
proto-general receiver of Flanders (Philip of Bourbourg) to the 1372 division of the
general receivership into two distinct offices.
Comital income came from disparate sources (domainal production, tolls, taxes on
concessioners, fines, reliefs, confirmations of privileges and rights, sales, loans) and passed
through the hands of various local agents or even persons deputized ad hoc. Payments
could be fixed in amount or not; they could be made at regular intervals or haphazardly.
