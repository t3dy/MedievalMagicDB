"It's a Kind of Magic”: Juggling Privacy and Prosecution for Sixteenth and Seventeenth Century 
English Magical Practitioners 
 
Sanne de Laat and Dan Harms 
 
Abstract 
 
Practitioners of ritual magic in sixteenth and seventeenth-century Britain faced both legal and 
social dangers, yet continued to engage in their activities with the assistance and support of 
other members of society. Doing so necessitated the acquisition and maintenance of privacy, 
even if it must often be breached to ensure success. After a discussion of ritual magicians’ 
privacy in both indoor and outdoor settings, this chapter examines scrying, a subtype of ritual 
magic in which the magician gains knowledge by means of looking into a reflective surface. 
Both manuals of magic, and popular and legal accounts of such rituals, display how magicians 
aspired to privacy under challenging social and spatial conditions, with varying degrees of 
success.  
 
“Thou that workst them, be secret in them.”1

result in serious penalties, both social and legal, to those conducting a ritual or participating in it. 
Those who wished to be successful at magic in early modern England had to be successful at 
negotiating the levels of privacy they required and desired. 
 
Setting the Boundaries 
 
Before engaging in this discussion of privacy in sixteenth and seventeenth-century manuscripts 
of ritual magic from England ritual magic, it is best to delve into what we mean by “privacy” and 
“ritual magic.”

group to choose to include or exclude others from an activity (or possibly mental state) in which 
they are engaged.3 Huebert provides four early modern English-language usages of “private”

“Ritual magic,” conversely, is not a category taken from the era; indeed, “magic” itself was a 
term used for wildly different activities and with disparate connotations. Klaassen identifies 
“ritual magic” as “practices involving lengthy ritual operations,” to which we might add that these 
often involve securing the assistance of various intermediary beings (such as angels, demons, 
fairies, the dead, or other spirits of unspecified nature), often through coercion, to provide 
operative benefits either to the magician or others.6 Common elements of ritual magic include 
purifications, almsgiving, prayers to God or holy beings, incantations to conjure or adjure spirits, 
consecrated items, protective circles and other diagrams, and spoken or written passages of 
“voces magicae,” often appropriated from Greek, Hebrew, or Arabic sources.

During the medieval period in Europe, ritual magic was often practiced within the “clerical 
underworld,” a network of learned men trained for the priesthood but often forced or choosing to 
take on less reputable activities for their own ends. When Henry VIII formed the Church of 
England, turning out many priests from their holdings and dissolving the monasteries, many 
individuals with magical books and knowledge had to seek other professions - including the 
practice of ritual magic. Many of the aspects that were mentioned in incantations or parts of 
rituals - various types of Mass, references to the Pope and Limbo - were explicitly Catholic in 
nature, and copyists employed various strategies with regard to these, whether leaving them 
unchanged or removing or marking over them. Yet magic and Catholicism became connected in 
discourse, re-emphasizing the need to keep such practice private.8 
 
Given that ritual magic could be directed toward hunting treasure that belonged to the crown, 
acquiring sexual favors, and harming others - including royalty - the state sought to prohibit 
rituals that were thought dangerous. Parliament passed acts against various aspects of ritual 
magic in 1542 (Henry VIII), 1563 (Elizabeth I), and 1604 (James I). The Henrician act explicitly 
discussed the various items a magician could possess, but all three acts instead focused on the 
use of the items rather than their possession. The Elizabethan and Jacobean acts also 
introduced nuanced wording, and a first offense was no longer automatically a death sentence. 
The ownership of books of spells was not prohibited, simply the use of particular portions of 
their contents.9 One should bear in mind that there seem to have been few prosecutions under

Yet the ritual power the state sought to constrain was also a source of a magician’s income and 
reputation. Acquiring new or variant rituals necessitated a magician’s participation in scribal 
networks through which written rituals could be exchanged. Some operations required or 
recommended the participation of associates, leading to more individuals being brought into the 
magician’s confidence. Finally, purveying magical services required the magician to divulge 
information about their purported capabilities to clients.  
 
This chapter will examine the precautions, difficulties, and urgency of privacy within two types of 
sources on ritual magic: the descriptive, including ego-documents, court cases, and popular 
accounts; and the prescriptive, including manuscript and printed instructions for a magician to 
follow. Each brings its own set of challenges for analysis. Descriptive documents, for instance, 
are valuable in their accounts of how magic was practiced “on the ground,” yet those that are 
not ego-documents are often written from uninformed or hostile perspectives, and in any case 
always reflect a breach in the privacy surrounding the action. Prescriptive documents do not 
always correspond to, and may lag behind, known accounts of practice and thus might skew 
perspectives on privacy in this period. Nonetheless, they not only present the possibilities a 
magician might have pursued, but also reflect the worldviews, interests, and aspirations of the 
copyists or owners of these documents.

Privacy in Ritual Magic 
 
Those who wished to keep their affairs private encountered serious challenges in this period. 
The prevalence of shared social spaces, including the bedroom, the cramped nature of many 
accommodations, and the porous nature of the architecture of the time allowed for both open 
and surreptitious observation.11 And this was simply for keeping business affairs or sexual 
liaisons secret – not for summoning spirits, a process that could involve lengthy incantations, 
drawing circles, and brandishing swords, wands, crystals, and other tools. This secrecy is a 
major requirement of many magical procedures; one “myst be very secret so that no man knowe 
thy entent & purpose” and that “what soever is seene or harde… must not be discovered to anie 
other earthly creature”.12 
 
The privacy necessary to perform a particular rite could vary widely, based upon its 
requirements. Folger V.b.26(2) includes a ritual against toothache, consisting of repeatedly 
writing the famous “Abracadabra” phrase on parchment, each time reducing it by one letter.13 
Such a creation would only take a few minutes and could be easy to conceal. On the other 
hand, other rituals required extensive preparations. A magical circle drawn on the ground might 
take a considerable amount of effort and time to create and would be difficult to explain if 
discovered. Some accounts describe circles that are large enough to hold two or three people, 
and one manual notes that two circles must be drawn “distaunt… 3 5 or 9 foote” from each

Some rituals had to be performed in an appropriate place, severely narrowing a magician’s 
choices. For example, a magician seeking treasure might have to perform the ritual at a site 
where riches were supposedly hidden, as the cunning woman Mary Parish and the MP Goodwin 
Wharton did on several occasions.15 Those who sought to consult the spirits of the dead would 
learn from Agrippa’s Three Books of Occult Philosophy that necromancy could only be 
performed in the place where the dead body or the affections of the deceased rested, “or by 
reason of some affection imprinted in their life, drawing the soul it self to certain places, or by 
reason of some hellish nature of the place.”16 Other magic might rely on the sanctity of a church, 
such as the “book and key” ritual William Lockesmythe performed in St. Audoen’s parish church 
with the assistance of the vicar.17 In other cases, a magician might be granted more scope as to 
where a ritual might occur – but doing so required them to navigate through the challenges to 
early modern privacy. 
 
In this milieu, indoor space to perform magic would have been difficult to negotiate. Most people 
shared their living space with others, whether family members or servants, with even sharing a 
bed being a common occurrence. If one wanted to have a private conversation, the best space 
might be the gallery, if the house’s owner was wealthy enough to handle one, or the garden.18 
Still, even these were only suited for quiet discussion, not for drawing a circle on the floor or 
ground, burning incense, and calling out incantations for considerable periods of time.

Yet more freedom could be found elsewhere, as ”…real privacy, especially for illicit activities, 
was, until well into the seventeenth century, most often represented as readily attainable only

whether into a garden or into uncultivated areas, to avoid observation. Magicians might have an 
additional reason to seek unfrequented areas: the preference of the spirits. The Folger 
manuscript tells us that “spirits are more willinge to appeare in some wast place, as in woddes,

Privacy for Scryers

played out, both in magical manuals and in practice. All one would need to practice scrying was 
a reflective surface, such as a mirror, a crystal ball or other gemstone, or even a thumbnail.

Nonetheless, scrying was also explicitly condemned in the so-called Witchcraft Acts. Although

A close reading of John Dee’s diary provides one of the best examples. Dee, relatively well off 
for most of his life and one of the most famous Elizabethan magicians, kept extensive diaries of 
which the majority has resurfaced. These diaries provide a clear insight into his struggles and 
successes with magic, but they also show how privacy and secrecy are tied into magic. He often 
includes people in his practices, utilizing Edward Kelley as his scryer and inviting Lord Albert 
Łaski as an additional participant.42 However, Dee also struggled with setting and maintaining 
his boundaries for the privacy he needed. 
Dee’s diary mentions several instances in which he is interrupted while in the midst of a scrying 
experiment. Dee shutting the doors to his office meant he required privacy, but he often forgot to 
shut those doors - the situation that led to the unwanted intrusion of Tanfeld and the angel’s dire 
prediction as to his fate.43 So, while Dee was able to set and maintain some sort of privacy, 
these boundaries were not always fully respected or maintained.

The examples mentioned above show magicians negotiating privacy so that they might avoid 
prosecution and yet be able to earn a living and reputation off their magic. In the magical 
manuals, privacy or secrecy might have been emphasized, but in practice scryers did not 
always maintain the level of privacy they might have needed, sometimes due to others’ 
interference, sometimes due to their choice to bring new people and outsiders into their magic 
practices. Further, while they valued their own privacy, they often breached that of others.  
 
Conclusions and Questions 
For a magician in this milieu, every ritual could become a potential compromise of privacy with 
problematic and even dangerous consequences. Still, depending upon the ritual, the resources 
available, and the magician’s ingenuity and prudence, ritual magic could be a viable and 
sometimes profitable practice. Nonetheless, failures in privacy did occur; ironically, the 
documentation this created reveals the high value magicians placed upon it, and the great 
lengths to which they went to create and preserve it. 
The examination above should only be considered preliminary in nature, touching as it does on 
only a small amount of the documentary evidence from a limited geographical region and time 
frame. Magicians across western and central Europe read the same authorities, participated in 
the same scribal networks, and encountered restrictions on their practice, so a broader survey 
might yield a richer and more nuanced tapestry on this topic. Likewise, a great deal of continuity 
existed in magical practice, not only between the late medieval and early modern periods, but 
also in later periods, with such individuals as Frederick Hockley and William Dawson Bellhouse

undertaking scrying rituals similar to those of their predecessors.54 In Britain, the 1735 
Witchcraft Act redefining fortune-telling as fraud and its repeal in 1951 would have changed the

The disconnect between the desire for privacy and the desire to breach that of others raises 
questions about whether magicians feared that members of their own vocation would breach 
their privacy with magic. It seemed to have been possible, as Humphrey Gilbert and John Davis 
scried upon themselves performing a magical operation in order to refine their techniques in 
1567.56 Were magicians concerned about magical surveillance of their activities, as well as the 
more mundane methods of breaching their privacy? 
It might be possible to advance a hypothesis on one item: the presence in the manuals of rituals 
requiring extensive preparations and stressing requirements for privacy that might have been 
difficult or impossible to obtain. Given the challenges described already, some rituals as 
described in these manuals may be aspirational, insofar as they could only be performed by 
those who already had access to privacy - freedom of their space, access to isolated locations, 
inside and out, and associates who would be discreet and secretive - requiring resources that 
must have been out of their reach.  
Were some of these rites written with no intent that they be practiced? Without a statement from 
the authors of these anonymous ritual magic manuscripts, this seems nearly impossible to 
determine. Still, not every ritual had these requirements, and a magician would be able to adapt 
the material in order to circumvent some of these requirements. Thus, the magician had access 
to rituals that might be able to provide the wealth, esteem, and secrets necessary to perform 
these other rites. Thus, the practice of magic becomes an iterative process, where the
