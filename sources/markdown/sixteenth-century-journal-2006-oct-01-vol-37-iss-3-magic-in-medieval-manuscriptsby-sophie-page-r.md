Sophie Page, a lecturer in the department of history at University College London, 
specializes in European medieval magic and astrology 
while exploring among other fields 
medicine, cosmology, and orthodox religion. Author of Astrology in Medieval Manuscripts 
(London: British Library, 2002), Page presents in her Magic in Medieval Manuscripts another 
facet of the medieval world. Her study is divided into an introduction 
and five chapters. Page 
opens her analysis with a brief overview of magic during the Middle Ages, revealing the 
diverse aspects of the supernatural starting 
with the definition of the term "magic" as con 
ceived during the Dark Ages. She continues with a presentation 
of the diverse forms and tra 
ditions of magic: herbs, incantations, 
charms, amulets, and so forth. 
Through an exploration of colorful images and texts from 
British Library manuscripts, 
Page, in her first 
part, entitled "The Medieval Magician," depicts an insightful 
representation 
of the ecclesiastic position regarding the supernatural 
world and the tensions arising between 
magical and miraculous occurrences. The conflict between the saint and the magician, or as 
Page states, "the competition for the role of prophet or performer of wonders" (9), is well 
exemplified 
with excerpts and illustrations 
taken from several 
manuscripts such as Brunetto 
Latini's Livre de Tresor (ca. 1266) and Jehan de Wavrin's Anchiennes Cronicques d'Engleterre 
(1442-44). In particular, Page details a few magicians' practices such as those of Merlin, 
Nectanebus, Circe, Medea, Queen Fredegunda, or the magicians of Xanadu encountered 
by Marco Polo through his travels. Interestingly, 
Page marks the distinction between male 
and female practitioners 
of magic and reveals their different 
techniques and expertise.

This collection of thirteen essays makes an important contribution to the study of 
Renaissance dialogue, a somewhat neglected genre and technique that has only recently 
come back into the focus of scholars of early modern literature. 
The volume's approach is 
interdisciplinary, 
which has the advantage of defining the dialogue as a pan-European phe 
nomenon, drawing on a multitude of literary traditions 
and languages that end up entering 
into a virtual dialogue themselves; a process that nicely underscores the significance of the 
topic on a structural level. The most obvious setback is the necessity to leave out many sem 
inal texts and to limit the study 
mostly to texts that are actually written in the form of a dia 
logue, thus eliminating a broad range of "dialogic" works that are not dialogues in the 
traditional sense (one might, for instance, think of Rabelais's prologues in that context) and 
that show the far-reaching impact of the technique. The editors seem well aware of such 
problems, however, as it was not their intention to offer an exhaustive study of dialogue and 
dialogism but merely a stimulating 
contribution to the topic that will foster 
more research, 
reflection, 
and dialogue, an undertaking in which they have succeeded admirably.
