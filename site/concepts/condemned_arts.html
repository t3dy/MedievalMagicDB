Condemned Arts

HYBRID / LEGAL

Definition

Actor Term (Latin: artes prohibitae, artes damnabiles, artes illicitae). The condemned arts designates the category of practices formally prohibited by canon law, theological condemnation, and inquisitorial procedure in medieval Christendom. The canonical taxonomy derives primarily from Isidore of Seville's Etymologiae VIII.9, transmitted through canon law (Gratian's Decretum, c.1140, Canon 26, q. 5) and theological summas. The condemned arts include divination in all its forms, demonic conjuration, maleficium (harmful magic), and all commerce with spiritual beings other than God and the saints.

The Canonical Taxonomy

Isidore's list of condemned arts became the standard reference for medieval moralists and canonists: magi (magicians), malefici (evildoers who work through demonic power), mathematici (astrologers who predict the future), haruspices (diviners from entrails), augures (from birds), pythones (those who speak through demons), and arioli (those who seek answers from demons through sacrifices). These categories were repeatedly rehearsed in penitentials, canon law collections, and theological treatises, and provided the vocabulary for inquisitorial inquiry.

The legal codification in Gratian's Decretum made these prohibitions enforceable in ecclesiastical courts. The development of inquisitorial procedure in the thirteenth century provided the institutional mechanism for their enforcement. The fifteenth-century elaboration of the witchcraft concept added the collective sabbath and explicit demonic pact to the list, generating a new wave of prosecutorial activity.

The Problem of Natural Magic

The boundary between the condemned arts and legitimate natural philosophy was the central contested terrain of medieval magical discourse. Natural magic — the use of occult properties of things without demonic assistance — was not straightforwardly condemned; the question was whether the specific operation under examination involved demonic cooperation or purely natural causes. Scholastic natural philosophy's investment in clarifying this boundary produced the taxonomic refinements of Albertus Magnus, Thomas Aquinas, and the Speculum astronomiae, which attempted to carve out a domain of licit natural magic within and adjacent to the broader category of the condemned arts.
