Demonology

ANALYST_TERM / DEMONOLOGICAL

Definition

Hybrid Term. Demonology designates both the historical study of demons conducted by theologians, philosophers, and learned magicians (actor-usage: the discipline of knowing demons) and the modern scholarly subdiscipline that studies such historical accounts (analyst-usage). In the medieval and early modern periods, demonology was a serious theological enterprise with practical stakes: if demons were real, what were their powers and limitations? Could they create genuine effects or only illusions? Could they be compelled by human art? These questions bore directly on the legal and theological assessment of magic, witchcraft, and popular protective ritual.

Historical Development

Christian demonology inherited a complex legacy from Jewish angelology, Neoplatonic daemon theory, and Pauline pneumatology. Augustine of Hippo's treatment of demons in De civitate Dei (VIII–X) and De divinatione daemonum established the foundational claims: demons are real, fallen angels with limited but genuine causal powers; they can appear to predict the future because they have better sensory equipment and faster movement than humans; they can cause apparent wonders but not true miracles; and all traffic with demons — even seemingly beneficial consultation — is illicit because it involves worship of beings other than God.

Medieval scholastic demonology elaborated this framework. Thomas Aquinas's Summa theologiae (especially the Treatise on Angels in the Prima Pars and the discussion of superstition in Secunda secundae qq. 92–96) provided the authoritative synthesis: demons are intellectual substances, created good but fallen through pride; their powers are real but limited by divine permission; they can affect material things (weather, disease, illusion) but cannot override free will or create genuine miracles. This framework licensed a highly specific taxonomy: natural effects (even extraordinary ones) remained within the domain of natural philosophy; genuinely miraculous effects belonged to God; demonic effects occupied a middle category of apparent wonder or praestigium.

The witch trials of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries generated a new genre of demonological treatise — from the Malleus Maleficarum (1486) through Bodin's Démonomanie des sorciers (1580), Remy's Demonolatreiae (1595), and del Rio's Disquisitionum magicarum (1599–1600) — that elaborated the concept of the demonic pact and the witches' sabbath as legal and theological categories. These texts are less concerned with the learned magical tradition (with which they overlap) than with the prosecution of popular magic understood as diabolical alliance.

Scholarly Resources

Stuart Clark's Thinking with Demons: The Idea of Witchcraft in Early Modern Europe (1997) provides the fullest analysis of early modern demonological thought as an intellectual system. Michael Bailey's Battling Demons (2003) traces the construction of the witch-sabbath concept in the fifteenth century. For the scholastic tradition, see Jan Machielsen (ed.), The Science of Demons: Early Modern Authors Facing Witchcraft and the Devil (2020). For ancient and Jewish demonology, Gideon Bohak and the relevant sections of Philip Alexander's work remain essential.
