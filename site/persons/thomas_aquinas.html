Thomas Aquinas

THEOLOGIAN / MEDIEVAL

Life

Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), born into a noble family at Roccasecca near Aquino in southern Italy, entered the Dominican Order in 1244, studied under Albertus Magnus at Cologne (1248–1252), and spent his career teaching at Paris and in Italy. He died at the monastery of Fossanova while travelling to the Council of Lyon. He was canonized in 1323 and declared a Doctor of the Church in 1567; his Summa theologiae was placed alongside the Bible on the altar at the Council of Trent. His theology and philosophy dominated the Catholic university curriculum from the thirteenth century to the twentieth.

Scholastic Demonology and Magic

Aquinas's treatment of magic appears principally in three locations: the Prima Pars of the Summa theologiae (on angels and demons as intellectual substances with limited causal powers), the Secunda secundae (qq. 92–96, on superstition, divination, and the pact with demons), and his shorter treatise De occultis operibus naturae (On the Occult Works of Nature), which addresses the natural philosophical limits of non-supernatural causation.

The Summa theologiae II-II, qq. 92–96 provide the most systematic scholastic condemnation of divination and magic as illicit forms of inquiry. Aquinas distinguishes superstition (which attributes to creatures — stars, images, demons — what belongs to God alone) from legitimate religious practice. Divination is always illicit when it involves implicit or explicit demonic pact; the desire to know the future by consulting anything other than legitimate causes or divine revelation is in itself a turning toward demonic power, even if the practitioner does not explicitly intend it. This framework made even passive or "natural" divination theologically suspect, and shaped the Inquisition's approach to magical practice for centuries.

On the natural philosophical side, De occultis operibus naturae argues that natural things can have properties exceeding ordinary causation (occult qualities) that produce apparently marvellous effects without supernatural intervention. This argument, against those who too quickly attributed unusual natural phenomena to demonic agency, licensed a domain of natural magical phenomena that could be studied and used without moral hazard. Combined with the condemnation of demonic magic, this position creates the characteristic Thomistic double gesture: nature can produce marvels; but any operation that requires demonic cooperation is illicit regardless of the intended end.
