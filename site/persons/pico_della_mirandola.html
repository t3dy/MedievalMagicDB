Giovanni Pico della Mirandola

PHILOSOPHER / RENAISSANCE

Life

Giovanni Pico della Mirandola (1463–1494) was born into the ruling family of Mirandola in Emilia, studied canon law at Bologna, philosophy at Ferrara and Padua, and Arabic and Kabbalah in Florence and Perugia. He was a prodigy in multiple languages (Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Arabic, Aramaic) and in philosophy, and his short career — he died at thirty-one, possibly of poisoning, in the year the French invaded Italy — produced works of extraordinary ambition and originality. He was closely associated with Ficino's Florentine milieu but also a distinctly independent and more radical thinker.

The 900 Conclusiones and Christian Kabbalah

Pico's Conclusiones nongentae (900 Conclusions, 1486) — 900 theses drawn from ancient, medieval, and contemporary sources, including 72 Kabbalistic and 26 "magical" theses — were intended for a public debate in Rome that Pope Innocent VIII refused to permit. Thirteen of the theses were condemned as heretical; Pico fled to France, was arrested, and returned to Florence under Medici protection. The condemned theses included claims that Kabbalistic art confirmed the divinity of Christ and that no science demonstrates the resurrection more effectively than natural magic and Kabbalah.

Pico's contribution to the history of magic was twofold. First, he added Christian Kabbalah to the Renaissance occult synthesis as the highest form of magical technology: manipulating the Hebrew divine names and sefirot could accomplish what natural magic and astral magic could not, because it operated at the level of the divine intellectual world rather than the elemental or celestial. Second, his Oratio de dignitate hominis (Oration on the Dignity of Man, composed as a preface to the 1486 debate but not delivered) articulated the vision of the magus as the person who unites all the worlds within himself — elemental, celestial, intellectual — and thereby achieves a form of deification. This vision became the standard Renaissance image of the philosopher-magician.

Later Work

After his condemnation and pardon, Pico moved toward a more sceptical position on magic: his posthumously published Disputationes adversus astrologiam divinatricem (Disputations against Divinatory Astrology, 1496) constitutes one of the most systematic Renaissance attacks on judicial astrology, anticipating many of the arguments that Kepler and later sceptics would make. His Heptaplus (1489) and De ente et uno (1492) represent a more orthodox Neoplatonist theology, less explicitly magical than the Conclusiones.
