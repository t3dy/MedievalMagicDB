Frank Klaassen

SCHOLAR / MODERN

Life and Scholarship

Frank Klaassen is a historian of medieval magic and learned culture at the University of Saskatchewan. His research focuses on the practical ritual magic texts of medieval England — the manuscripts that circulated among literate clergy and university-trained practitioners — and on what their content and manuscript contexts reveal about the culture of learned magic in the later Middle Ages. He studied at the University of Toronto and completed his doctorate there.

The Transformations of Magic

The Transformations of Magic: Illicit Learned Magic in the Later Middle Ages (Penn State University Press, 2013) is Klaassen's major monograph and the most rigorous systematic study of the English learned magic manuscript tradition. The book surveys approximately 270 manuscripts containing ritual magic material, providing a quantitative foundation for generalizations about what types of magic circulated, in what social contexts, and how the tradition changed between c.1300 and c.1500. Klaassen identifies two transformations: a shift in some parts of the tradition toward more explicit demonic conjuration, and a counter-trend of naturalization in which ritual operations were increasingly framed within the idiom of scholastic natural philosophy and celestial influence theory.

The book's methodological contribution — the systematic manuscript survey rather than focus on a few canonical texts — was influential in subsequent grimoire scholarship, demonstrating that the selection bias of printed editions had significantly distorted earlier accounts of what medieval learned magic actually looked like.

Other Work

Klaassen has edited and introduced key primary texts in the medieval magic corpus. His edition (with Christopher Phillips) of the Ars Notoria tradition and his contributions to Claire Fanger's Invoking Angels (Penn State, 2012) expand the manuscript base for understanding angelic magic in the medieval period. He has also published on the relationship between learned magic and early modern print culture, on the transition from manuscript to print in the magical tradition, and on the Solomonic attribution as a legitimating strategy.
