Johannes Hartlieb (c. 1400–1468): Court Physician and Expositor of Forbidden Arts

Johannes Hartlieb was a Bavarian court physician and author who served the Wittelsbach dynasty and produced one of the most significant vernacular treatments of magic in fifteenth-century Germany. His Buch aller verbotenen Kunst, Unglaubens und der Zauberei (Book of All Forbidden Arts, Superstition, and Sorcery, c. 1456) provided a systematic vernacular taxonomy of magical practices organized around the traditional schema of the seven forbidden arts—nigromancy, geomancy, hydromancy, aeromancy, pyromancy, chiromancy, and spatulamancy—supplemented by discussions of other condemned practices including witchcraft and demonic compacts.

Hartlieb's text is particularly valuable for historians because it was written for a courtly lay audience rather than for academic theologians, reflecting the dissemination of anti-magical discourse beyond the Latin-literate clerical and university world. His patron, the Margrave Johann von Brandenburg, received a text that combined moralizing condemnation with practical description, giving readers enough information about magical practices to recognize and avoid them while making the material accessible through vernacular German prose rather than scholastic Latin. This pedagogical strategy—explaining in order to condemn—characterized much early vernacular demonological literature and inadvertently preserved detailed descriptions of magical practices.

As a physician, Hartlieb occupied a professional position at the intersection of natural philosophy, medicine, and magic. Medical practitioners of the fifteenth century regularly encountered questions about the boundary between natural cures, learned magic, and popular healing practices, and Hartlieb's exposition of forbidden arts likely drew on his professional experience with cases where patients had sought magical remedies. His work thus illuminates both the learned discourse about magic and the social reality of magical practice in late medieval German urban and courtly culture, providing a perspective that complements the more abstract theoretical treatments produced by theologians and inquisitors.
