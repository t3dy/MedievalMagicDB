Herman Gollancz (1852–1930): Scholar of Hebrew and the Magical Tradition

Herman Gollancz was a British rabbi and scholar of Semitic languages whose edition of the Sepher Maphteah Shelomoh (Book of the Key of Solomon) and other related texts made important contributions to the scholarly study of Jewish magical literature and its relationship to the broader Western grimoire tradition. Working in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Gollancz represented a generation of Jewish scholars who brought rigorous philological methods to the study of Hebrew and Aramaic texts that had been either ignored or sensationalized by earlier generations of Christian Hebraists.

His editorial work on Solomonic magical texts required navigating the complex manuscript traditions of Hebrew grimoires, which existed in multiple recensions reflecting centuries of copying, translation, and adaptation. The Hebrew Solomonic tradition—including the Mafteah Shelomoh, the Sepher ha-Razim, and related compilations—occupied an important position in the history of Western magic because it represented both an independent Jewish magical tradition and a potential source (through Latin translation) for European learned magic. Establishing reliable texts of these works was therefore significant not only for Jewish studies but for understanding the transmission channels through which Jewish magical concepts entered medieval Christian culture.

Gollancz's scholarship exemplified the broader professionalization of Jewish studies in Britain and Europe during the late Victorian and Edwardian periods, a development that transformed the academic study of Jewish texts from a largely Christian theological enterprise into a philologically rigorous discipline practiced by Jewish and non-Jewish scholars alike. His contributions to magical text studies remain relevant to contemporary scholarship, which continues to investigate the multilingual, multicultural transmission networks through which magical knowledge circulated across the medieval Mediterranean and European worlds.
