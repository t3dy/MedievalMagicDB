David Pingree

SCHOLAR / MODERN

Life and Scholarship

David Pingree (1933–2005) was Professor of History of Mathematics and History of Science at Brown University and the most productive editor and translator of mathematical, astronomical, and magical texts in the Western historical tradition. His output — spanning Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic, Greek, and Latin — was extraordinary in its scope and precision. He is described by colleagues as having produced more critical editions of primary sources in the history of science and astrology than any other scholar of his generation.

Work on the Magical Tradition

For the history of magic, Pingree's most important contributions are his critical editions of the Picatrix (2 vols., Warburg Institute Studies 39, 1986) — the standard scholarly edition of both the Latin text and the related Arabic tradition — and of Thabit ibn Qurra's De imaginibus, al-Kindi's astrological works, and numerous other texts in the astrological-magical tradition. His edition of the Picatrix established the manuscript tradition, identified the Arabic sources, and provided the essential tools for all subsequent scholarship on the most comprehensive medieval text of astrological magic. Without Pingree's editions, systematic scholarly work on the Arabic-Latin transmission of astral magic theory would be impossible.

Pingree also produced critical editions of several Sanskrit astrological texts, Persian astronomical tables, and Greek astrological compilations, demonstrating through these comparative editions the transmission routes through which ancient Babylonian and Hellenistic astronomy and astrology reached Arabic, Persian, and eventually Latin learning. His contributions to understanding the exact bibliographic and textual transmission paths of magical and astrological knowledge set the standard for subsequent work in the field.

Legacy

Pingree's students and successors — including Charles Burnett, Julio Samsó, and others working on the Arabic-Latin transmission — continue the philological tradition he established. His Picatrix edition, in particular, remains the indispensable scholarly resource for anyone working on astrological magic theory in the medieval Latin tradition.
