The Transformations of Magic

SCHOLARSHIP / MODERN / ENGLISH

Overview

Frank Klaassen's The Transformations of Magic: Illicit Learned Magic in the Later Middle Ages (Penn State University Press, 2013) is the most rigorous analysis of what Klaassen calls the "experiment literature" — the practical ritual magic books (nigromantic handbooks, ars notoria texts, Solomonic conjuration manuals) that circulated in manuscript among clerically trained readers in medieval England and Continental Europe from approximately 1300 to 1500. The book argues against two misleading models: the model that treats medieval magic as a unified, coherent tradition with stable content, and the model that treats the learned magical tradition as a pale copy of, or preparation for, Renaissance Hermeticism. Instead, Klaassen documents what actually survived in English manuscripts and shows how the content, purposes, and social contexts of learned magic shifted substantially over two centuries.

Argument and Evidence

Klaassen's central methodological contribution is the systematic survey of a defined corpus: he identifies and analyses approximately 270 surviving manuscripts containing ritual magic material in the medieval English tradition, rather than relying on a handful of well-known texts. This allows him to distinguish majority practice (ars notoria texts, image magic, simple conjurations) from exceptional cases (full demonic pact rituals), to trace the social circulation of texts across different manuscript contexts, and to identify two distinct types of transformation across the later medieval period.

The first transformation Klaassen identifies is a shift from "Solomonic" ritual magic — which emphasised the magician's moral purity, his Solomonic authority, and the coercive binding of spirits within a broadly Christian ritual framework — toward a more openly demonic conjuration literature in which the pact and explicit appeal to demonic power become more visible. The second transformation, running in the opposite direction, is the gradual absorption of Solomonic and image magic into the emerging idiom of "natural philosophy": Klaassen documents how later medieval readers and scribes increasingly framed magical operations in naturalistic terms, aligning them with Albertan and Arabist accounts of occult properties and celestial influence, effectively naturalising what earlier manuscripts had treated as operations of religious power.

Significance for Grimoire Studies

The book makes three major contributions to the study of the grimoire tradition. First, by documenting the manuscript evidence rather than relying on printed editions, Klaassen corrects the distortions introduced by the selective survival and editorial intervention of printed magical texts. Second, by demonstrating that "the learned magical tradition" is not a single thing but a set of practices and texts that changed substantially across two centuries, the book warns against the anachronistic projection of later Renaissance or early modern magical theory back onto medieval texts. Third, by focusing on what scribes and readers actually did with manuscripts — how they copied, excerpted, combined, and reorganised texts — Klaassen provides a model of manuscript-culture analysis that influenced subsequent work on the Ars Notoria, the Munich Manual, and Solomonic texts.

Context and Reception

Klaassen's work was preceded by and engages throughout with Richard Kieckhefer's Forbidden Rites (1997), which edited and analysed the Munich Manual of demonic magic, and with Claire Fanger's edited volumes on medieval ritual magic. It has been widely cited in subsequent scholarship on the grimoire tradition, including Dan Harms and Joseph Peterson's work on specific texts and Sophie Page's studies of magical manuscripts in institutional collections.
