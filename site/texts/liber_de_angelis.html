Liber de Angelis: Medieval Angel Magic and Practical Conjuration

The Liber de Angelis (Book of Angels) is a medieval Latin magical compilation preserved in several manuscript versions from the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, providing practical instructions for invoking and working with angels for a variety of purposes including healing, protection, acquiring knowledge, and influencing persons and events. The text occupies the contested territory between legitimate Christian veneration of angels and condemned magical conjuration, claiming divine and angelic sanction for its procedures while providing operational instructions whose practical orientation connects it to the broader learned magic tradition.

The text draws on the rich medieval tradition of angelology that assigned specific names, powers, and spheres of responsibility to vast hierarchies of angels derived from Jewish, Christian, and ultimately Babylonian and Persian cosmological speculation. The association of specific angels with days, hours, planets, and zodiacal signs—elaborated in texts like the Sepher ha-Razim, the Heptameron, and countless liturgical and paraliturgical sources—provided practitioners with a systematic framework for identifying which angel to address for which purpose and when. The Liber de Angelis applied this angelological knowledge in explicitly operational directions, providing the names, seals, and ritual procedures for actual invocation.

Scholarly work on texts like the Liber de Angelis has been part of a broader effort to map the full range of medieval learned magical literature beyond its canonical exemplars. Researchers including Julien Véronèse, Claire Fanger, and Frank Klaassen have demonstrated that the medieval magical corpus encompasses a far greater variety of texts—in terms of content, purpose, and intended audience—than earlier scholarship recognized. The Liber de Angelis contributes to this picture by providing evidence for the persistence and practical application of angel magic traditions in contexts where practitioners sought benevolent spiritual assistance rather than the compulsion of demons.
