The Magic of Rogues

EDITION / MODERN / ENGLISH

Magic of Rogues: The Underworld of Late Medieval Magic

The study of what might be called "rogue magic"—magical practices operating outside the learned clerical tradition, in the spaces of urban popular culture, traveling entertainers, confidence men, and social margins—reveals dimensions of medieval and early modern magical culture that the learned tradition's self-representation often conceals. Richard Kieckhefer's analysis in Magic in the Middle Ages and related work, along with studies by Michael Bailey and others, has attended to the intersection of learned and popular magic in social contexts where the boundaries between legitimate and condemned practice were negotiated in everyday life rather than in academic treatises.

The figure of the professional magician—the itinerant practitioner who offered magical services for payment, sometimes drawing on genuine learned texts and sometimes fabricating claims to knowledge—occupies an important but elusive place in the history of magic. Such figures appear in court records, in satirical literature, and in moralizing condemnations, but rarely left self-descriptions that would allow reconstruction of their actual practices. The overlap between their operations and those of more respectable practitioners (court astrologers, university-trained physicians, learned clerics who wrote magical texts) was considerable, suggesting that the social boundary between legitimate and illegitimate magic was less about the content of practices than about the social position and institutional affiliation of practitioners.

The study of rogue magic intersects with broader social histories of poverty, migration, gender, and religious marginality in medieval and early modern Europe. Women healers, cunning folk, itinerant charm-sellers, and others who operated outside institutional structures of learning and ecclesiastical authority practiced forms of magic that drew on the same cosmological assumptions as learned magic while operating through different social channels. Understanding this dimension of magical culture requires sources—court records, ecclesiastical visitiation documents, folk narrative collections—that complement the learned texts typically studied by historians of magic, producing a more complete picture of magic as a social and cultural phenomenon.
