De radiis

TREATISE / MEDIEVAL / LATIN

Overview

Al-Kindi's De radiis stellarum (On Stellar Rays) — surviving only in a medieval Latin translation, its Arabic original apparently lost — is the most theoretically systematic account of magical causation in the Arabic-Latin intellectual tradition and the primary source for the naturalistic theory that underlies astrological and talismanic magic. Probably written around 850 CE and translated into Latin in the twelfth century, the text argues from first principles that all causation in the cosmos operates through the radiation of qualities from every existing thing, and that this universal radiation system provides the mechanism by which magic works.

The Theory of Rays

Al-Kindi argues that every entity in the world — celestial and terrestrial, material and immaterial — continuously emits rays in all directions, analogous to the emission of light from a luminous body. These rays carry the specific qualities of their source and affect the things they strike, changing their qualities, movements, or states. Celestial bodies emit the most powerful and pervasive rays, because their nature is superior and their position gives them access to the entire sublunary world. But terrestrial things also emit rays; the human will and imagination, when intensely concentrated, can emit rays that affect other things at a distance — this is the theoretical basis for the magician's power.

The magician who understands the ray-system can work within it: by concentrating his will on a specific intention, using materials and words that resonate with specific celestial sources, timing his operations to celestial configurations that strengthen the relevant rays, he produces effects at a distance through purely natural (if hidden) causes. Al-Kindi is thus the philosopher of natural magic in its most rigorous form: magic is applied natural philosophy, and the boundaries between the two are established by knowledge rather than by essential difference.

Influence

The influence of De radiis on Latin natural philosophy was extensive. Thabit ibn Qurra's De imaginibus applies the ray theory to astrological image magic. Robert Grosseteste's metaphysics of light and Roger Bacon's multiplication of species draw on related principles. The Speculum astronomiae cites al-Kindi's works on images; Nicolas Weill-Parot's analysis of addressative magic situates De radiis as the theoretical foundation that subsequent scholastic discussion of image magic either accepted, qualified, or rejected. David Pingree's catalogue of al-Kindi's works provides the bibliographic context.
