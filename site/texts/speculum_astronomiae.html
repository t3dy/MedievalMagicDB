Speculum astronomiae

TREATISE / MEDIEVAL / LATIN

Overview

The Speculum astronomiae (Mirror of Astronomy), attributed to Albertus Magnus though the attribution has been contested (Nicolas Weill-Parot argues against it; most scholars accept it), is the most systematic scholastic treatment of the boundary between legitimate and illegitimate astrological magic composed in the thirteenth century. Probably written in the 1260s, the text was addressed to the growing number of natural philosophers who were encountering Arabic astrological image magic and needed guidance on which procedures were compatible with Christian orthodoxy and which were not.

The Tripartite Classification

The Speculum's central contribution is a tripartite classification of astrological image-making. The first category — fully licit — comprises images that work through purely natural celestial causes: talismans whose figures, materials, and timing are keyed to the natural influences of specific planets, operating as material focal points for stellar power in the way that a burning glass concentrates light. The second category — potentially licit, depending on content — comprises images that may or may not involve demonic invocation: the key question is whether the text that accompanies the image explicitly addresses spiritual agents by name (thereby becoming "addressative" in Weill-Parot's terminology) or merely exploits natural stellar influence. The third category — clearly illicit — comprises images that explicitly invoke demonic names or require demonic cooperation: these are condemned without qualification.

This classification had significant consequences for subsequent natural philosophy and magic. It legitimated a domain of astrological talisman-making as natural philosophy, provided a framework for evaluating specific texts and procedures, and influenced later Renaissance defenders of natural magic (Ficino, Agrippa, della Porta) who sought to work within the licit category. It also identified the key diagnostic question — whether a magical procedure explicitly addresses spiritual agents — that Nicolas Weill-Parot's concept of addressative magic systematizes and applies to the broader history of medieval astrological image magic.
