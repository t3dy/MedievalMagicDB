GRIMOIRE / EARLY_MODERN / LATIN

Ars Goetia: The Seventy-Two Demons of Solomon

The Ars Goetia (or simply the Goetia) is the first and most famous book of the seventeenth-century compilation known as the Lemegeton or Lesser Key of Solomon. It presents a systematic catalogue of seventy-two demons whom Solomon allegedly bound under his seal and confined in a brass vessel, together with detailed descriptions of each spirit's appearance, rank, powers, and the method of compelling it to appear and perform service. The spirits are organized hierarchically into kings, dukes, princes, marquises, presidents, earls, and knights, with each rank commanding specified numbers of lesser spirits and possessing particular areas of competence ranging from knowledge of hidden things to the teaching of arts and sciences to the creation of illusions.

The catalogue's origins lie in earlier medieval demonological literature. Several of the spirit names and their attributed powers show connections to the Jewish Testament of Solomon, to Arabic magical compilations, and to the expanding demonological encyclopaedias assembled by late medieval learned magic practitioners. The specific list of seventy-two, however, appears to crystallize in the sixteenth century, with manuscript versions circulating before the compilation of the Lemegeton. Johann Weyer's Pseudomonarchia Daemonum (1577), appended to his De praestigiis daemonum, provided an early printed list of these spirits that served as a source for subsequent compilations including the Goetia itself.

The Goetia became enormously influential in the modern Western magical tradition following Aleister Crowley and Samuel Liddell Mathers' edition of 1904, which combined the manuscript text with Crowley's extended commentary and additional material. This edition transformed a seventeenth-century manuscript compilation into a foundational text of twentieth-century ceremonial magic. Historical scholarship, including the work of Joseph Peterson and Daniel Harms, has subsequently worked to reconstruct the earlier manuscript traditions and disentangle the authentic seventeenth-century text from later accretions, establishing the Goetia's place within the broader history of Solomonic magic rather than treating it primarily through the lens of modern occultism.
