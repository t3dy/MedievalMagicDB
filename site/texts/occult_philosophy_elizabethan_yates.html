The Occult Philosophy in the Elizabethan Age

SCHOLARSHIP / MODERN / ENGLISH

Overview

Frances Yates's The Occult Philosophy in the Elizabethan Age (Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1979) examines the role of Hermetic and Kabbalistic thought in Elizabethan England, focusing on figures including John Dee, Christopher Marlowe, Shakespeare, and Edmund Spenser as participants in a broadly Hermetic-Rosicrucian intellectual culture. The book extends the argument of Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition into English literary and intellectual history, arguing that the occult philosophy permeated Elizabethan culture at a deep level.

Argument and Assessment

Yates argues that the Elizabethan period saw the climax of the Hermetic-Kabbalistic synthesis in England, particularly through John Dee's influence on court culture and intellectual circles. She traces connections between Dee's mathematical and magical programme, the Sidney circle's Neoplatonism, Marlowe's Doctor Faustus as a meditation on the magician's fate, and the allegorical structure of Spenser's Faerie Queene. Her identification of a coherent "occult philosophy" pervading Elizabethan culture was characteristic of her synthetic ambition.

The book has been received more cautiously than the Bruno study. The specific connections between Dee and the literary figures Yates discusses have often been questioned; Glyn Parry's more sceptical political reading of Dee (The Arch-Conjuror of England, 2011) and Nicholas Clulee's philosophical analysis of Dee's natural philosophy (John Dee's Natural Philosophy, 1988) have both provided frameworks that engage with Yates's account while qualifying its broader claims. Nevertheless, the book remains valuable for its documentation of the circulation of Hermetic ideas in Elizabethan England and for its attention to the literary dimensions of occult philosophy.
