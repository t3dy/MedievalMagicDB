Clavicula Salomonis

GRIMOIRE / MEDIEVAL / LATIN

Overview

The Clavicula Salomonis (Key of Solomon) is the most widely circulated and extensively copied of the medieval and early modern Solomonic grimoires, surviving in over one hundred manuscripts in Latin, Italian, Hebrew, French, and English, ranging from the fourteenth century to the nineteenth. It is attributed to Solomon himself or to the transmission from Solomon to his son Roboam, and presents itself as a comprehensive practical manual for the conjuration of spirits, the construction of magical implements, and the performance of a wide range of ritual operations.

Structure and Contents

The text is typically organized in two books. Book I covers the practical ritual operations: how to conjure spirits to achieve specific ends (finding hidden treasures, obtaining love, causing invisibility, healing disease, inflicting harm). Book II covers the preparation of the magician and his instruments: the days and hours of the planets under which various operations should be conducted, the making and consecration of the magic circle, the pentacles attributed to the seven planets (each accompanied by a figure and a conjuration), the preparation of magical tools (knife, wand, lamen, robe, etc.), and the ritual of suffumigation and prayer.

The manuscript tradition is enormously complex. Joseph Peterson's online edition (Esoteric Archives) documents over fifty Latin and vernacular manuscripts; his printed edition (Weiser, 2004) provides the most thorough textual analysis available. S.L. Mathers's 1889 edition, based on British Museum manuscripts, was the first modern printed edition and shaped the occult revival's understanding of the text, though it combined several divergent manuscript families without adequate documentation.

Historical Development

The earliest datable manuscripts of the Clavicula Salomonis are from the fourteenth century; the text clearly assembled over a long period from multiple source traditions, incorporating elements of older Solomonic demonology, astrological image magic, and ritual prayer. The Hebrew Mafteah Shelomoh (Key of Solomon) tradition is related but may represent an independent development rather than a simple translation from Latin. The Arabic tradition of Solomonic conjuration (Solomon as Sulayman, master of jinn) provides a parallel context rather than a direct source. Frank Klaassen's work on the medieval English manuscript tradition and Pablo Torijano's on the ancient background remain the principal scholarly frameworks.
