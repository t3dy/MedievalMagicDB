De Occulta Philosophia

TREATISE / RENAISSANCE / LATIN

Overview

De occulta philosophia libri tres (Three Books of Occult Philosophy) by Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa von Nettesheim was first circulated in manuscript from around 1510, circulated widely in that form through networks connecting the Rhine valley humanists to Italian Neoplatonists, and finally published in printed form in 1531 (Cologne, Johannes Soter). A heavily revised version appeared the same year in Antwerp. The work is the most ambitious encyclopedic synthesis of Renaissance occult philosophy: a three-book structure corresponding to the three worlds of Neoplatonic cosmology — the elemental world, the celestial world, and the intellectual world — and providing a comprehensive account of the theory and practice of natural, celestial, and ceremonial magic.

Structure and Contents

Book I (Natural Magic) covers the occult properties of the four elements, the sympathies and antipathies among things in the elemental world, the medical and magical uses of plants, stones, and metals, the doctrine of occult qualities (following pseudo-Albertus and al-Kindi), the role of the imagination and passions in magical operations, and the theory of the world-soul as the mediating principle through which magical influence travels.

Book II (Celestial or Mathematical Magic) addresses the seven planets and their influence on temperament and character, the construction of astrological talismans and magical images, the magical squares (camea) attributed to each planet (with their numerological and geometric properties), the magical significance of numbers, proportions, and geometric figures, and the doctrine of the fixed stars and their images.

Book III (Ceremonial or Divine Magic) is the most contentious and the most theologically ambitious. It treats the angelic and demonic hierarchies in their relationship to the divine names; the Kabbalah as a technology of divine name manipulation; the power of words, letters, characters, and seals; and the operations by which the magus might ascend through the celestial hierarchy to communicate with angels, culminating in the figure of the homo divinus who unites the three worlds in a single act of mystical gnosis. This book draws heavily on Pico della Mirandola, Reuchlin's Kabbalistic works, and Trithemius.

Sources and Method

The erudition deployed across the three books is remarkable. Agrippa synthesizes Ficino's De vita and Platonic Theology, Pico's Conclusiones, the pseudo-Albertian natural magic treatises, al-Kindi's De radiis, the Picatrix, Proclus, Iamblichus, the Corpus Hermeticum, Pseudo-Dionysius, the Sefer Yetzirah, and hundreds of other sources from ancient, Arabic, Jewish, and Christian traditions. The organization is not merely encyclopedic but argumentative: Agrippa wants to demonstrate that all these traditions converge on a unified account of how occult power operates in the universe, and that a properly trained philosophical magician can work within this system without demonic pact.

Manuscript Tradition and Printed Editions

The manuscript versions of De occulta philosophia circulated from 1510 onward, with a copy sent to Trithemius (who responded cautiously but approvingly) and others reaching Erasmus's circle. The differences between the manuscript versions and the 1531 printed text are significant: Agrippa removed some of the more provocative material on ceremonial magic and added a preface emphasizing the work's natural-philosophical orientation. The 1531 Antwerp edition incorporates further revisions. The 1651 English edition (attributed to J.F.) is the version most familiar to Anglophone occultists but contains numerous translation errors and was accompanied by the spurious Fourth Book of Occult Philosophy, which has caused confusion about the limits of Agrippa's authorship.

Scholarly Reception

Charles Nauert's Agrippa and the Crisis of Renaissance Thought (1965) remains the standard scholarly biography and remains essential for understanding the De occulta philosophia in context. Brian Copenhaver's work on Renaissance magic has repeatedly returned to Agrippa as a central case study in the relationship between Neoplatonic philosophy and practical magic. Wouter Hanegraaff's Esotericism and the Academy (2012) treats the work as a founding text in the Western esoteric tradition. For the manuscript tradition, see the studies of V. Perrone Compagni, who produced the critical edition (Brill, 1992).
