Valerie Flint

SCHOLAR / MODERN

Life and Scholarship

Valerie I.J. Flint (1936–2009) was a historian of medieval religion and culture at the University of Hull and later the University of Auckland, whose work challenged the dominant historiographical model of the early medieval Church as simply suppressive of pagan and magical practices. Her scholarship argued for a more complex and ambivalent relationship between institutional Christianity and the popular magical practices it officially condemned.

The Rise of Magic in Early Medieval Europe

The Rise of Magic in Early Medieval Europe (Princeton University Press, 1991) is Flint's major work and one of the most provocative interventions in the historiography of medieval magic. Flint argues that the early medieval Church did not simply condemn and suppress popular magic: it deliberately accommodated, Christianized, and in some respects promoted forms of magical practice that it reframed as compatible with orthodox Christianity. Healing rituals, protective charms, divination, and even some forms of spirit-consultation were tolerated or transformed rather than eliminated, because the Church needed to win converts from precisely the social world where such practices were embedded.

The argument rests on close reading of pastoral texts, penitentials, canon law collections, and hagiography from the sixth through eleventh centuries. Flint shows how the same practices (using written texts for healing, consulting the calendar for lucky and unlucky days, employing protective objects) appear in both condemned and approved contexts, and argues that the difference is often one of authorization and framing rather than content. A charm using the Lord's Prayer rather than a pagan deity's name is structurally identical to its pagan predecessor but theologically transformed.

Reception and Significance

Flint's thesis was controversial: critics argued that she overstated the degree of ecclesiastical endorsement and understated genuine suppression. Richard Kieckhefer, Karen Louise Jolly, and others have engaged with and refined her argument. Her work is significant for insisting that the relationship between institutional Christianity and popular magic was not simply one of condemnation and resistance, and for drawing attention to the pastoral and missionary contexts within which early medieval attitudes toward magic were formed.
