Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa

AUTHOR / RENAISSANCE

Life

Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa von Nettesheim (1486–1535) was born in Cologne into a minor noble family. His education at the University of Cologne gave him the Latin learning and Scholastic philosophy that underpin his major works, while his subsequent wandering career — soldier, diplomat, court physician, lecturer, and perennial debtor — exposed him to the intellectual currents of humanist Italy, French and English royal courts, and the reform milieu of early German Lutheranism. He served in Maximilian I's campaigns in northern Italy around 1511, where contact with Italian Platonists (including possibly pupils of Ficino's circle) sharpened his interest in occult philosophy. He lectured on Reuchlin's Kabbalistic works at Dôle in 1509 and at a young age completed a first draft of his encyclopedic De occulta philosophia.

De occulta philosophia

The De occulta philosophia libri tres, first circulated in manuscript from 1510 onward and finally published in 1531 (Cologne, Soter), is the most comprehensive synthesis of Renaissance occult philosophy: a structured encyclopedia covering all three "worlds" of the Neoplatonic cosmos. Book I treats natural magic — the occult properties of things in the elemental world, the four elements, the harmony of the macrocosm and microcosm, the magical use of stones, plants, metals, animals, and numbers. Book II treats celestial or mathematical magic — the influences of the seven planets, the fixed stars, and the signs of the zodiac; the construction of magical images and talismans; magical squares, the camea of the planets. Book III treats ceremonial or divine magic — the celestial hierarchy of angels and demons, divine names, the Kabbalah, the power of words and letters, ritual operations for drawing down divine assistance.

The work synthesizes an enormous range of sources: Ficino's Neoplatonism and astral medicine, Pico's Kabbalah, Trithemius on cryptography and angelic communication, pseudo-Albertus on occult properties, the Hermetic writings, Pliny, and Arabic astrological theory via al-Kindi and the Picatrix. Its ambition was not merely encyclopedic but philosophical: Agrippa wanted to demonstrate that a unified science of nature, properly understood, encompassed what the ignorant dismissed as superstition.

De incertitudine and the Sceptical Turn

The paradox of Agrippa's career is that his other major published work, De incertitudine et vanitate scientiarum et artium (1530, published slightly before De occulta philosophia), appears to repudiate everything the larger book argues for. De incertitudine is a Pyrrhonist satire attacking all human knowledge, including magic: Agrippa denounces the whole project of occult philosophy as vanity, asserting that only Scriptural revelation provides genuine knowledge. The relationship between the two works has been debated for centuries. Charles Nauert's Agrippa and the Crisis of Renaissance Thought (1965) remains the standard monograph. Paola Zambelli's work reassessed Agrippa's scepticism in the context of fideist theology and rhetorical strategies of self-protection.

Influence

Agrippa's influence was enormous and long-lasting. De occulta philosophia was reprinted throughout the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries; English versions circulated in manuscript before the 1651 printed edition (which included the spurious "Fourth Book"). John Dee owned copies and annotated them; the Elizabethan magus Simon Forman and others drew on it directly. Christopher Marlowe almost certainly drew on the Agrippa legend for Doctor Faustus (c.1592). The Fourth Book of Occult Philosophy, a demonic conjuration manual falsely appended to Agrippa's name, circulated from the 1550s, further complicating his posthumous reputation. His reputation as an extreme magician persisted long after his death.
