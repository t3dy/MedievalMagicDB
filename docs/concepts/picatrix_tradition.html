Picatrix Tradition

HYBRID / ASTRAL

Definition

Analyst Term. The "Picatrix tradition" designates the influence of the Picatrix (Arabic Ghāyat al-ḥakīm, Goal of the Wise; Latin translation c.1300) on the subsequent history of astrological image magic in medieval and Renaissance Europe. The Picatrix was the most comprehensive and theoretically sophisticated astrological magic text available in Latin, and its influence on the learned magical tradition — through direct citation, through its provision of the theory of talismanic efficacy, and through its catalogues of materials and procedures — was pervasive from the fourteenth century onward.

Direct Influence

The most direct instances of Picatrix influence include: Marsilio Ficino's use of its astral medicine material in De vita coelitus comparanda (1489), where several specific procedures for attracting planetary spiritus are adapted from the Picatrix; Cornelius Agrippa's incorporation of its planetary correspondence tables and talisman procedures in De occulta philosophia II (1531); and the various astrological magic compilations of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries that drew on its procedures for specific operations. The Picatrix's organization around the twenty-eight lunar mansions — a finer-grained system than planetary magic alone — spread through subsequent talisman literature.

Nicolas Weill-Parot's analysis of addressative magic situates the Picatrix tradition at the centre of the scholastic controversy over image magic: the Picatrix's procedures explicitly address planetary spirits as personal agents, invoking them by name and requesting their cooperation — precisely the "addressative" character that distinguished illicit demonic magic from licit natural talisman operation in the scholastic taxonomy. David Pingree's critical edition (1986) and the Attrell-Porreca English translation (2019) are the scholarly foundations for studying the Picatrix tradition.
