Witchcraft

ANALYST_TERM / DEMONOLOGICAL

Definition

Hybrid Term. "Witchcraft" in scholarly usage designates both the historical belief — widely documented across cultures — that certain persons can cause harm (maleficium) through supernatural means, and the specific late medieval and early modern elaboration of that belief into the concept of the diabolical witch: a person who has made an explicit pact with the Devil, attends nocturnal sabbaths, engages in infanticide and cannibalism, and systematically inverts Christian religious practice. These two senses are related but distinct; conflating them distorts the history of both.

Maleficium and the Common Tradition

The older, more cross-culturally widespread belief involves maleficium (harmful magic): the attribution of disease, crop failure, livestock death, and other misfortune to the hostile magical action of a neighbour or enemy. This belief — documented in Roman law, early medieval canon law collections, and throughout medieval pastoral literature — constitutes the "common tradition" of popular magic in Kieckhefer's framework. The practitioner accused of maleficium need not be conceptualized as a Satanic witch; accusations of maleficium are structurally similar to accusations of witchcraft in non-Christian societies and do not inherently involve diabolism.

The Construction of the Diabolical Witch

The concept of the diabolical witch — the specific demonological construct that drove the major witch trials of the fifteenth through seventeenth centuries — was a learned, clerical construction. Michael Bailey's Battling Demons (2003) traces its emergence in the early fifteenth century from the confluence of three elements: the inquisitorial prosecution of heresy (which provided the concept of diabolical conspiracy), the pastoral concern with maleficium and popular magic, and the learned demonology of scholastic theology. The Malleus Maleficarum (1486) codified the construct: the witch explicitly renounces Christianity, makes a pact with the Devil, attends the sabbath, and performs maleficia through demonic power.

Stuart Clark's Thinking with Demons (1997) analyses early modern demonological writing as an internally coherent intellectual system rather than as mere superstition. Richard Kieckhefer's work, Richard Gordon's studies, and the essays collected in Bengt Ankarloo and Stuart Clark (eds.), Witchcraft and Magic in Europe (6 vols., Athlone/Penn State, 1999–2002), provide comprehensive coverage.
