Pseudepigraphy

ANALYST_TERM / MANUSCRIPT

Definition

Analyst Term. Pseudepigraphy (from Greek pseudepigraphos, "falsely inscribed") designates the attribution of a text to an author who did not write it — a practice that, in the ancient and medieval worlds, served functions quite different from modern literary fraud. In the contexts most relevant to the history of magic, pseudepigraphic attribution to figures such as Solomon, Hermes Trismegistus, Aristotle (via the Secretum Secretorum), or Moses provided the attributed text with an authority it could not claim on its own merits: the antiquity, wisdom, royal power, or prophetic status of the named author transferred to the text and legitimated its contents.

Functions in the Magical Tradition

Solomonic pseudepigraphy is the most important instance in the history of the grimoire. Texts such as the Testament of Solomon, the Clavicula Salomonis, the Key of Solomon, the Lemegeton, and dozens of subsidiary texts claim to preserve the actual magical knowledge of the biblical king — the names and seals he used to bind demons in the construction of the Temple, the rings and pentacles with which he controlled spirits, the adjurations he taught to his sons or to Roboam. This attribution strategy accomplished several things simultaneously: it provided a pre-Christian, divinely sanctioned ancestry for procedures that might otherwise appear demonic; it anchored the text in biblical history, making its authority hard to dismiss without impugning Solomon himself; and it supplied the practitioner using the text with a vicarious identification with Solomon's royal, priestly authority.

Hermetic pseudepigraphy (attributing texts to Hermes Trismegistus) served parallel functions in the natural-philosophical magical tradition. The Corpus Hermeticum, the Asclepius, the Emerald Tablet, and the Picatrix all claim ancient Egyptian or Hermetic authority. The 1463 Ficinian translation of the Corpus Hermeticum launched a tradition that lasted until Casaubon's 1614 philological dating of the texts to the first three centuries CE.

Scholarship

Pablo Torijano's Solomon the Esoteric King (2002) is the primary analysis of Solomonic pseudepigraphy as a cross-cultural phenomenon. For Hermetic pseudepigraphy, Garth Fowden's The Egyptian Hermes (1986) and Brian Copenhaver's introduction to his Hermetica translation (1992) are foundational. John Monfasani has written on Aristotelian pseudepigraphy. For the broader phenomenon in late ancient and medieval literary culture, see Michael Stone, David Aune, and the entries in the standard encyclopedias of pseudepigraphy.
