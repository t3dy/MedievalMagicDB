Kabbalism in Renaissance Magic

HYBRID / HISTORIOGRAPHICAL

Definition

Analyst Term. "Kabbalism and Renaissance magic" designates the synthesis of Jewish Kabbalah with Christian theology and Neoplatonic philosophy achieved by Pico della Mirandola, Johannes Reuchlin, and Cornelius Agrippa in the period 1486–1531, which produced "Christian Kabbalah" — an adaptation of Jewish mystical doctrines about divine names, sefirot, and letter-permutation serving as a technology of magical and theological knowledge. Christian Kabbalah provided the third element of the standard Renaissance occult synthesis: natural magic (elemental), astral magic (celestial), and Kabbalistic magic (divine or intellectual).

Pico and the Beginnings

Giovanni Pico della Mirandola's 900 Conclusiones (Rome, 1486) included 72 Kabbalistic theses, among them the claim that no science demonstrates the divinity of Christ more effectively than Kabbalah. Pico learned Kabbalah from Jewish teachers — primarily the convert Samuel ben Nissim Abulfaraj (Flavius Mithridates) — and purchased a collection of Kabbalistic manuscripts. His Apologia (1487), written to defend the condemned theses, argued that Kabbalah was not demonic magic but a divinely revealed mystical tradition confirming Christian truth. His distinction between "practical Kabbalah" (the magical manipulation of divine names) and "speculative Kabbalah" (theological meditation) mapped onto the Christian distinction between licit and illicit enquiry, with Pico firmly placing his own interest in the speculative category.

Reuchlin and Agrippa

Johannes Reuchlin's De verbo mirifico (1494) and De arte cabalistica (1517) provided more systematic accounts of Christian Kabbalah for German humanist readers, focusing especially on the divine name (the Tetragrammaton) and its Christological interpretation. Cornelius Agrippa's De occulta philosophia III (1531) incorporated Kabbalistic materials systematically into the third level of his magical hierarchy — divine or ceremonial magic — arguing that the manipulation of Hebrew divine names could draw down angelic assistance and ultimately achieve direct divine communication.

Scholarship

François Secret's Les Kabbalistes chrétiens de la Renaissance (1964) is the foundational survey. Joseph Dan's work on Kabbalah and early modern Christianity, and Moshe Idel's comparative studies of Kabbalah and magic, provide the Jewish scholarly perspective. For Agrippa, see Charles Nauert's biography (1965) and V. Perrone Compagni's critical edition of De occulta philosophia (Brill, 1992).
