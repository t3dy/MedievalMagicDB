Experimental Science

ANALYST_TERM / HISTORIOGRAPHICAL

Definition

Actor Term (Roger Bacon's scientia experimentalis) and Analyst Term. Roger Bacon coined the term scientia experimentalis in the Opus maius (c.1267) to designate the highest branch of natural philosophy — the science that verifies the conclusions of the other sciences through direct experience and produces the most powerful practical effects from knowledge of nature's hidden causes. The relationship between Bacon's experimental science and magic is both close and contested: Bacon explicitly naturalized many phenomena that others attributed to magic, arguing that they resulted from natural processes accessible to careful investigation.

Bacon's Programme

Part VI of the Opus maius on scientia experimentalis identifies three prerogatives of experimental knowledge: it certifies the conclusions of the other sciences (only experience can confirm or refute theory); it adds new discoveries not available through syllogistic reasoning; and it produces marvellous arts that may seem magical to the uninitiated. This third prerogative — the production of wonders through natural knowledge — directly overlaps with natural magic: Bacon mentions the possibility of explosive powder (anticipating gunpowder), optical instruments that magnify distant objects, ships that move without oarsmen, and flying machines, all as products of experimental science rather than magic.

Bacon's stance on actual demonic magic was unambiguous: he condemned it as inferior to true natural knowledge because it depended on deceptive spiritual agents rather than genuine understanding of causes. But his naturalization of many apparently magical phenomena — through his theory of multiplication of species (the mechanism of celestial influence), his optics, and his account of the imagination's power — substantially expanded the domain of what natural philosophy could claim.

Historiographical Significance

Lynn Thorndike's History of Magic and Experimental Science (1923–1958) treated Bacon as a key figure in the gradual separation of experimental science from magic. David Lindberg's subsequent work on Bacon's optics and natural philosophy has provided a more nuanced account of his theoretical framework and its dependence on Arabic sources (Alhazen, al-Kindi). The relationship between Bacon's programme and early modern experimental science is debated: he had no direct institutional successors, and his practical programme was not realized until long after his death.
