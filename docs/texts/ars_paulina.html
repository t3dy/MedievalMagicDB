Ars Paulina

GRIMOIRE / EARLY_MODERN / LATIN

Ars Paulina: Angelic Governors of Hours and Degrees

The Ars Paulina forms the third book of the Lemegeton compilation and represents a sophisticated integration of astrological and angelic lore. The text describes two classes of angels: those governing the twenty-four hours of the day (Book One), and those governing the 360 degrees of the zodiac (Book Two). Unlike the demonic conjuration of the Goetia, the Pauline Art claims to operate through angels who are benevolent ministers of the celestial order, accessible to practitioners who know their proper names and the astrological conditions under which they exert influence. The text attributes its transmission to the apostle Paul, citing Paul's account in 2 Corinthians of being "caught up to the third heaven"—an attribution that claimed apostolic authority for practices that the institutional Church would otherwise condemn.

The text's astrological framework connects it to the broader tradition of astrological magic elaborated in texts like the Picatrix and the writings of Thabit ibn Qurra. The assignment of angels to specific hours and zodiacal degrees reflects the medieval understanding of celestial influence as operating through hierarchically ordered spiritual beings who transmitted planetary or stellar virtue to the sublunary world. This conceptual framework allowed magical practitioners to present their operations as working in harmony with—rather than against—the divinely ordained cosmic order, since invoking the angel of a particular celestial configuration was ostensibly different from commanding demons.

The Pauline Art's place within the Lemegeton compilation illustrates the synthetic character of seventeenth-century learned magic: the assembler drew together texts of diverse origin—demonic conjuration, angelic invocation, talismanic magic, the Notory Art—and presented them as components of a unified Solomonic system. This synthetic presentation was itself a form of intellectual legitimation, suggesting that the various operations constituted parts of a coherent magical philosophy rather than a miscellaneous collection of techniques. The attribution to Paul, like the attribution to Solomon elsewhere in the compilation, served to anchor the entire system in ancient sacred authority.
