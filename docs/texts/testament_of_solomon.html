Testament of Solomon

PRIMARY_SOURCE / LATE_ANTIQUE / GREEK

Overview

The Testament of Solomon is a Greek pseudepigraphic text, probably composed between the first and fifth centuries CE, that narrates how King Solomon, aided by a magic ring given by the archangel Michael, summoned and interrogated demons, learning their names, their functions, the afflictions they cause, and the angels capable of thwarting them. The demons are then set to work constructing the Temple in Jerusalem. The text is the earliest systematic demonology in Greek and the foundational text of the Solomonic grimoire tradition: its framework of named demons bound to named angels, organized by Solomonic authority, provides the template replicated across two millennia of magical literature.

Structure and Contents

The narrative frame has Solomon receive a ring from Michael, inscribed with a pentagram, that gives him power over demons. He then successively summons thirty-six demons (later expanded in some manuscript traditions), each of whom reveals his name and the specific affliction he causes. Typical entries follow a pattern: the demon appears, reveals his name (often etymologized or pun-connected to his function), describes the diseases or misfortunes he inflicts, and reveals the angel whose name, written or spoken, can neutralize him. Some entries include planetary associations, gemstone affinities, and brief ritual instructions.

The text exists in several manuscript recensions of varying length and arrangement: McCown identified five principal recensions in his 1922 critical edition. The most important manuscripts are in the Bibliothèque nationale de France and the Vienna National Library. Dennis Duling's translation in Charlesworth's Old Testament Pseudepigrapha (Doubleday, 1983) is the standard English version.

Historical Significance

The Testament of Solomon is significant for several reasons. First, as a demonological text, it represents the earliest comprehensive name-catalogue of demons with individual attributes — a structure replicated in the Ars Goetia (part of the Lemegeton), the Book of Abramelin, and dozens of medieval and early modern grimoires. Second, as a pseudepigraphic text, it establishes the attribution strategy that would legitimise the entire subsequent Solomonic corpus: magical knowledge is Solomonic in origin, transmitted by divine gift (Michael's ring), and serves ultimately to build the Temple — that is, to serve God's purposes. Third, its composite nature — drawing on Jewish, Christian, pagan Greek, and possibly Egyptian elements — makes it evidence for the multicultural magical milieu of the late antique Mediterranean.
