Spiritual and Demonic Magic: From Ficino to Campanella

SCHOLARSHIP / MODERN / ENGLISH

Overview

D.P. Walker's Spiritual and Demonic Magic from Ficino to Campanella, first published by the Warburg Institute in 1958 (and reissued in paperback by Penn State University Press, 2000), is the foundational scholarly study of Renaissance learned magic as a philosophical problem. Written before Frances Yates's more ambitious synthetic works, the book is more cautious in its causal claims and more attentive to the philosophical difficulties confronting Renaissance thinkers who sought to claim legitimate natural-philosophical authority for operations that resembled, and often derived from, the demonic magic condemned by medieval theologians. It remains required reading for any study of Renaissance occult philosophy.

Argument

Walker's central question is: how did Renaissance Neoplatonist magicians — above all Ficino and his successors — attempt to distinguish their operations from demonic magic, and how successful were these attempts philosophically and practically? The problem Walker identifies is structural: the theoretical framework through which Ficino explained the efficacy of his astral magic (the doctrine of the world-soul, pneumatic mediation, the sympathy of like for like) derived from Neoplatonic sources — Plotinus, Iamblichus, Proclus — that were not themselves entirely innocent of demonic implication. Iamblichus and Proclus had both discussed the role of daemons in ritual operations; the intermediary spirits of Neoplatonic theurgy shaded imperceptibly into the Christian demons that any orthodox theologian was required to condemn.

Walker traces the problem through a sequence of case studies: Ficino's De vita coelitus comparanda, Pico della Mirandola's attempt to distinguish Kabbalah from demonic magic, Francesco Giorgi's Harmonia mundi, Cornelius Agrippa's De occulta philosophia, Giambattista della Porta's Magia naturalis, the music-magic theory of Ficino's successors, and the later career of the tradition through Francesco Patrizi, Giordano Bruno, and Tommaso Campanella. In each case, Walker shows both the sophistication of the attempt to naturalize magical operations and the points at which the naturalization broke down — where the philosopher needed to invoke spirit-working that could not be fully distinguished from demonic cooperation.

Key Concepts

"Spiritual magic" in Walker's usage designates operations that work through the spiritus — the subtle pneumatic vehicle of the soul and the world-soul — and that their practitioners claimed were purely natural, operating through the sympathetic relationships between celestial and terrestrial things. "Demonic magic" designates operations that require explicit cooperation with demonic or angelic intelligences as rational, personal agents. Walker's argument is that the boundary between these two types was genuinely contested and philosophically unstable throughout the tradition he surveys.

Walker also contributed the concept of "spiritual magic as a problem of orthodoxy" — the observation that Renaissance philosophers were not simply operating in a pre-scientific context where magic was unproblematically acceptable, but were themselves aware of the theological dangers and sought to manage them. This insight corrected older historiography that presented Renaissance magic as naive belief, and anticipated more recent work on learned magic as a theoretically sophisticated practice.

Legacy

Walker's book was the immediate predecessor and in some respects the model for Frances Yates's work, and the relationship between them is worth noting. Walker's caution and his focus on philosophical tensions contrasts with Yates's synthetic ambition; scholars who found Yates's thesis too sweeping have generally returned to Walker's more cautious framework. Brian Copenhaver's philosophical analysis of Ficino builds directly on Walker; D.P. Walker's other work on music and magic (in essays collected in Music, Spirit and Language in the Renaissance, ed. Pennelope Gouk, 1985) extends the book's concerns. The Walker tradition — careful philosophical reading, attention to the natural/demonic distinction, sensitivity to the risks faced by Renaissance magical thinkers — remains the dominant approach in the field.
