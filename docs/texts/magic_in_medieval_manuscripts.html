SCHOLARSHIP / MODERN / ENGLISH

Magic in Medieval Manuscripts (2010): A Visual and Textual Survey

Sophie Page's Magic in Medieval Manuscripts (2010), published by the British Library, provides an accessible yet scholarly introduction to magical texts and their manuscript contexts through the British Library's extensive collections. The volume combines analysis of magical content with attention to the material and visual dimensions of magical manuscripts—their scripts, illustrations, diagrams, and physical form—demonstrating how the study of manuscripts as objects, rather than merely as vehicles for textual content, can illuminate the practices and communities that produced and used them. Page's expertise in medieval magic and manuscript culture makes the volume both an authoritative scholarly resource and an effective guide for general readers encountering this material for the first time.

The book's coverage ranges from the Anglo-Saxon period through the late Middle Ages, examining texts in Latin, Middle English, and other vernaculars. It addresses the full spectrum of magical literature from theoretical demonology and natural magic to practical grimoires and popular charms, situating each within its historical and cultural context. Particular attention is paid to the visual elements of magical manuscripts: the elaborate diagrams of the Ars Notoria, the spirit seals of Solomonic texts, the astronomical tables of astrological magic, and the illustrated charms of vernacular tradition. These visual components are integral to the magical function of the texts themselves, not merely decorative additions.

Page's approach illustrates a broader methodological turn in the study of medieval magic toward attention to the material dimensions of textual transmission. Rather than treating manuscripts as neutral containers for textual content, scholars in this tradition examine the physical properties of manuscripts—parchment type, binding, marginal annotations, signs of use—as evidence for how texts were read, stored, and transmitted. This material philology has revealed important information about the social contexts of magical text production and use, suggesting audiences and practices that analysis of content alone would miss. The British Library's collections, rich in magical manuscripts of all periods and traditions, make them an ideal platform for this kind of investigation.
