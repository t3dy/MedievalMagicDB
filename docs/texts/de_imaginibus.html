De Imaginibus: Thabit ibn Qurra and Talismanic Magic

The text known in Latin as De imaginibus (On Images) and attributed to the ninth-century Sabian mathematician and astronomer Thabit ibn Qurra represents one of the most influential transmissions of Arabic talismanic magic into the medieval Latin West. The text provides instructions for creating astrological talismans—images, figures, or inscribed objects—at specific celestial conjunctions to capture and channel planetary or stellar influences for a variety of practical goals including healing, protection, and the influencing of persons and events. Its detailed operational instructions, combined with astrological rationale, made it a key source for Latin authors seeking to engage with image magic within a natural philosophical framework.

The text's attribution to Thabit ibn Qurra, while questioned by modern scholars, reflects the medieval strategy of anchoring magical texts in authoritative ancient or prestigious near-contemporary names. Thabit's genuine reputation as a mathematician and astronomer of the first rank—he translated Greek mathematical texts into Arabic and made original contributions to astronomy and number theory—lent credibility to a text making bold claims about celestial influence. Whether or not Thabit wrote anything like the text attributed to him, the attribution circulated widely and influenced the text's reception as legitimate natural philosophy rather than condemned superstition.

The De imaginibus occupies a central place in Nicole Weill-Parot's important study of "addressative" magic—that is, magic based on the idea that images or objects can be addressed to specific spiritual or celestial entities capable of conferring power. Her analysis situates the text within the debate about whether image magic relies on natural celestial causation (and is thus philosophically defensible) or on the implicit invocation of spiritual beings (and is thus condemned as demonic). This debate, which runs through the entire medieval treatment of talismanic and astrological magic, gave the De imaginibus its controversial significance: it was both a resource for those who sought to develop a natural philosophical account of magic and a target for those who insisted that any such account merely obscured underlying demonic dependency.
