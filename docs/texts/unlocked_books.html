Unlocked Books

SCHOLARSHIP / MODERN / ENGLISH

Overview

Benedek Láng's Unlocked Books: Manuscripts of Learned Magic in the Medieval Libraries of Central Europe (Pennsylvania State University Press, 2008) extends the systematic study of magical manuscripts in institutional library collections — pioneered for England by Kieckhefer, Klaassen, and Page — to the libraries of Hungary, Austria, Poland, and Bohemia. The result substantially expands the geographic scope of medieval magic scholarship and demonstrates that the learned magical tradition was a genuinely pan-European phenomenon with significant regional variation.

Findings and Significance

Láng's survey of Central European library collections reveals that magical manuscripts were present in significant quantities in the major institutional libraries of the region: the royal library at Buda, the chapter libraries of Esztergom and Krakow, the university libraries of Vienna and Prague. The types of magical material represented span the full range of the European learned tradition: astrological and geomantic texts, the Ars Notoria, Solomonic conjuration, natural magic, and miscellaneous practical magical material.

The book's title reflects its central argumentative point: the magical manuscripts were "unlocked" — that is, accessible in regular library collections, available to any reader with access to the institution, not hidden in restricted or clandestine locations. This accessibility suggests that the social reality of magical manuscript possession and circulation was more open than the theological rhetoric of condemnation implies. Readers who consulted a magical text in a chapter library were not necessarily engaging in transgressive behaviour; they were using the resources of a learned library in the same way as they would use other technical or philosophical texts. Láng's findings complement Klaassen's for England and Page's for monastic contexts, establishing the "open library" character of medieval magical manuscript culture as a European-wide phenomenon.
